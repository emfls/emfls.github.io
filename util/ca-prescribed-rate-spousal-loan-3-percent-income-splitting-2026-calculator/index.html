Key takeaways

The CRA prescribed rate was 3.0% in all four quarters of 2026. A C$250,000 loan at 3% costs C$7,500 in interest for a full year. Whether income splitting saves tax depends on both spouses’ marginal rates, the types of return they earn, and how much interest the borrower can deduct.

Loan and portfolio inputs

Enter combined federal and provincial marginal rates on ordinary income. For Québec, include the federal abatement in your rates. The 1% rate was available for qualifying loans originated from July 2020 through June 2022.

Portfolio return mix

Enter the share of each year’s return. All three shares must add up to 100%.

January 30 payment check

January 30, 2027 falls on a Saturday. Allow enough time for the full payment to clear by the deadline. The check uses one full year’s interest on a constant principal.

How the estimate works

Without a loan, the lender pays tax on the portfolio return. With a qualifying loan, the borrower pays tax on the portfolio return and the lender pays ordinary income tax on loan interest. In the default setting, the borrower’s interest deduction reduces investment tax to a minimum of zero. Select the other-income option only if the borrower can deduct the full interest at the entered rate. See the CRA’s interest deductibility folio.

Break-even is the yield where first-year family tax is equal in both cases, accounting for any cap on the deduction. The estimate uses eligible dividend credits on taxable dividends of ON 10.06%, BC 12%, AB 8.12% and QC 11.7%. For Québec, it also adjusts the federal credit for the 16.5% abatement. Modeled dividend tax per dollar of eligible dividends has a zero floor.

Both portfolios reinvest annual after-tax returns. The interest payment is a transfer within the family, and the loan principal stays fixed. The projection uses constant rates, a non-negative yield and annual realization of gains. It excludes fees, bracket changes, foreign tax credits and benefit clawbacks. It estimates lost savings versus the no-loan baseline if the exemption fails. Any reassessment, interest or penalties depend on the actual tax returns and payment history.

Frequently asked questions

Can I keep the 3% prescribed rate after 2026?

Yes. For the prescribed-rate route, section 74.5(2) tests the rate in effect when the loan was made. Document the loan and its fixed rate in a signed promissory note, trace the funds to the investments, and pay all interest due on time every year. Confirm the origination rate for any legacy loan.

What if I miss the January 30 payment?

Pay the full 2026 interest by January 30, 2027 under the 30-day rule. Use a completed cash payment, cleared cheque or bank transfer and keep proof. A new promissory note or a journal entry alone does not count as payment in this check. Missing the deadline can attribute investment income and taxable gains to the lender for the missed year and later years on the same loan.

Can the borrower deduct the loan interest?

Interest may qualify under paragraph 20(1)(c) when the loan creates a legal obligation to pay interest and the borrowed funds are traceably used to earn income from property or a business. An investment bought solely for capital gains generally fails that test. The lender includes interest received as ordinary income.

How do eligible dividends and capital gains change break-even?

Canadian eligible dividends are grossed up by 38% and qualify for eligible dividend tax credits. Generally, 50% of realized capital gains is taxable. Interest and foreign dividends are fully included. These treatments change the effective tax-rate gap between spouses, which determines the break-even yield together with the interest and deduction.

What does the 10-year compounded figure measure?

It is the end-of-year-10 difference between two portfolios that both start with the same balance and reinvest their after-family-tax returns. The original loan principal and annual interest stay fixed. The cumulative tax difference adds the actual modeled annual tax gaps after the balances begin to diverge.
