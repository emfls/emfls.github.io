Key takeaways

In 2026, one donor can elect to spread up to $95,000 for one beneficiary over five tax years. Two spouses can reach $190,000 with two equal gifts or valid gift splitting and separate Form 709 elections. A full election can use $0 of the lifetime exemption when the annual exclusions cover all gifts to that beneficiary.

The federal K–12 cap doubles from $10,000 to $20,000 per beneficiary per year in 2026 across all 529 plans for eligible expenses. State deduction, credit, and withdrawal rules vary.

What the estimate assumes

Front-loading puts more money at risk sooner.

A market decline can leave the lump-sum strategy behind a gradual funding plan. If you need the money back for an ineligible expense, the earnings portion can face income tax, a 10% additional federal tax unless an exception applies, and possible state recapture. If a donor dies during the election period, the portion allocated to calendar years after the year of death generally returns to that donor’s gross estate.

  • Both strategies deposit the same dollars before age 18. Deposits occur at the start of each year and compound at the entered annual return after fees. The brokerage comparison taxes positive gains once at age 18 at the entered effective rate. It excludes yearly tax drag and tax benefits from losses.
  • State savings use constant entered rates and 2026 caps, sufficient eligible income for deductions, and the entered available liability for credits. Totals include usable carryforward after age 18 and exclude discounting, reinvestment, and recapture. The estimate assumes one account and one beneficiary. The full joint caps in Pennsylvania and Maryland assume both spouses qualify. Virginia uses the standard $4,000 cap.
  • Gift projections hold the 2026 annual exclusion constant in future years. Two-donor scenarios assume equal gifts or valid splitting, with equal other gifts. Prior elections and special exclusions require separate review. Gifts over the exclusion may use lifetime exemption or result in tax depending on the donor’s circumstances.

Frequently asked questions

How much can I superfund a 529 in 2026?

A donor can elect to spread up to $95,000 per beneficiary evenly over five tax years, starting in 2026. Two spouses can elect up to $190,000 in total with equal gifts or valid gift splitting. A full election leaves no room under a $19,000 annual exclusion, so other gifts during the election can use lifetime exemption.

Who files Form 709 for a 529 election?

Each electing donor files their own Form 709 for the contribution year. Spouses using gift splitting for a superfunding gift generally each file and consent. Later election-year allocations alone generally do not require a return when there is no other filing trigger.

What does the $20,000 federal K–12 limit cover?

Beginning in 2026, the limit applies per beneficiary per year across all 529 plans. It covers qualifying tuition and specified curriculum, books, online materials, tutoring, tests, dual enrollment fees, and educational therapies for students with disabilities. State tax treatment can differ.

Can annual contributions save more in state taxes?

Yes. A lump sum can use up a single year's deduction or credit and forfeit the excess where carryforward is unavailable. Annual deposits may qualify in several years. In Pennsylvania and Ohio, eligible contributions to any state's 529 can qualify.

What happens if a donor dies during the five-year election?

The elected contribution allocated to calendar years after the year of death generally goes into that donor’s gross estate. For example, a $95,000 election begun in 2026 generally leaves $38,000 for estate inclusion if the donor dies in 2028.
