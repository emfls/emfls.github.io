Comprehensive Guide to Canadian Construction Holdbacks & 2026 Reforms

In the Canadian construction sector, the statutory construction holdback is one of the most powerful legal instruments balancing commercial liquidity with trade contractor security. Codified across provincial lien statutes—principally the Ontario Construction Act (R.S.O. 1990, c. C.30) and the British Columbia Builders Lien Act (S.B.C. 1997, c. 45)—the law mandates that every payer in the construction hierarchy retain 10% of the value of services or materials supplied on every progress billing.

1. The Purpose and Structure of the 10% Statutory Holdback

The construction industry operates on a pyramid credit structure: project owners contract with general contractors (GCs), who engage specialized trade subcontractors, who in turn hire sub-subcontractors and purchase building materials from commercial suppliers. Because downstream trades provide valuable labour and materials on credit before being paid, the 10% holdback serves two critical statutory functions:

  • Capped Owner Liability: By retaining 10% of each invoice, the project owner establishes a statutory trust fund. If an insolvent general contractor fails to pay downstream subtrades, the owner’s maximum exposure to construction liens registered against the property title is strictly limited to that 10% holdback fund, provided the owner complied with all statutory retention rules.
  • Security Fund for Unpaid Trades: Subcontractors, suppliers, and construction workers hold statutory lien rights against the holdback fund, ensuring a guaranteed pool of capital is preserved to satisfy valid lien claims.

2. The Ontario January 1, 2026 Mandatory Annual Release Reform

Historically, one of the greatest financial burdens imposed on Canadian general contractors and civil engineering firms was the indefinite locking of holdback funds on multi-year infrastructure, commercial towers, and transit projects. Contracts lasting 3 to 5 years routinely trapped millions of dollars in 10% holdbacks until the entire project achieved substantial performance.

To eliminate this liquidity trap and curb predatory retention clauses, Ontario enacted historic amendments to the Construction Act taking effect on January 1, 2026. Under the 2026 statutory regime:

  • Mandatory Annual Release: For all construction contracts with a completion period exceeding one year (or contracts structured with phased milestones), holdback release is now mandatory on an annual basis. Project owners and developers can no longer contractually enforce clauses deferring holdback until final project completion.
  • Statutory Timeline and Form 6 Notice: Within 14 days following each contract anniversary date, the payer must publish a formal Notice of Contract Anniversary (Form 6) in a recognized construction trade newspaper. Provided no liens have been preserved, the owner must release the accrued 10% annual holdback within 60 to 74 days after notice publication.
  • 14-Day Mandatory Flow-Down: General contractors receiving released holdback funds must flow down corresponding payments to their subcontractors within 14 calendar days of receipt, ensuring liquidity immediately cascades through the supply chain.

3. Quantifying Contractor Carrying Cost Savings

Because contractors and trades must finance operating cash flow, payroll, and materials while 10% of their invoices are held back, they typically borrow against commercial bank lines of credit (often priced at Prime + 2.0% to 3.0%, or approximately 7.0% to 8.5% per annum). Releasing holdbacks annually instead of holding them until project termination dramatically reduces cumulative debt interest carrying costs:

4. British Columbia Builders Lien Act (The 55-Day Release Rule)

In British Columbia, holdback administration follows the rigorous procedural timeline of the Builders Lien Act:

  • Certificate of Completion (Form 3): Once a payment certifier issues a Certificate of Completion (or substantial performance is achieved), a 45-day statutory lien filing window commences. All trades must register their claims of lien within this 45-day window.
  • The 55-Day Holdback Release Date: The statutory holdback period officially expires 55 days after the Certificate of Completion is issued (providing a 10-day buffer following the 45-day lien period). If no claims of lien have been filed, the payer may safely release the full 10% holdback.
  • Mandatory Joint Trust Account (Section 5): On all private construction contracts where the contract value exceeds $100,000 CAD, the owner and general contractor must open a joint interest-bearing holdback trust account at a financial institution. The owner must deposit the 10% holdback into this account, which can only be operated with the joint signatures of both owner and contractor.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

1. Can an owner and contractor contractually waive the 10% holdback requirement?
No. Provincial construction lien legislation is public policy law. Section 5 of the Ontario Construction Act explicitly renders void any contract clause or agreement that purports to waive, diminish, or contract out of the 10% statutory holdback obligation.
2. Does the holdback apply to HST / GST on construction billings?
The holdback is calculated on the value of the services and materials supplied before sales taxes (HST/GST). However, when the holdback is subsequently released and invoiced, the applicable tax (such as 13% HST in Ontario or 5% GST in BC) must be accounted for according to Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) guidelines.
3. What happens if a subcontractor preserves a lien before holdback release?
If a claim of lien is preserved or registered against property title prior to holdback release, the owner must immediately suspend payment of the holdback. The owner can only release funds once the lien is discharged by payment, settled, or vacated by paying security into court under Section 44 of the Act.
4. What is a "Finishing Holdback" in Ontario?
Under Section 22(2) of the Ontario Act, if substantial performance is certified but minor deficient or incomplete items remain, the owner must release the main 10% holdback while retaining a separate 10% "Finishing Holdback" calculated solely on the estimated value of the remaining work.
