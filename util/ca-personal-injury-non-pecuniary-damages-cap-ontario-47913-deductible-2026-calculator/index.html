How the 2026 Ontario deductible works

Start with an estimate for the particular loss the court or the parties are valuing. In a personal injury claim, an award for non-pecuniary loss compensates for consequences such as physical pain, suffering, disability and loss of enjoyment of life. Evidence about what the injured person can do at home, at work and in their community can help explain the impact of an injury. A separate estimate for missed earnings or necessary future care belongs under the applicable economic head of damages. Keeping those amounts distinct is essential when testing whether a non-pecuniary award crosses Ontario’s monetary threshold.

Two provisions work together. Subsections 267.5(8) and (8.1) of Ontario’s Insurance Act create the monetary exemptions, and section 5.1 of Ontario Regulation 461/96 prescribes the indexed deductibles used for these modern Ontario automobile claims. The Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario (FSRA) publishes the figures. For 2026 the published adjustment is 2.4%. At the monetary threshold itself a qualifying award still attracts the deductible. A qualifying award one cent higher escapes the entire deduction. The statute also calls for the deductible to be applied before apportioning contributory negligence. Ontario Insurance Act, s. 267.5 · O. Reg. 461/96 · FSRA 2026 guidance, Appendix 1

First establish that the damages are recoverable. For a protected defendant, subsection 267.5(5) limits liability for non-pecuniary loss to cases involving death, permanent serious disfigurement, or permanent serious impairment of an important physical, mental or psychological function, subject to statutory exceptions. Regulation 461/96 sets out criteria and evidence requirements for serious impairment. A dollar figure above the monetary threshold cannot establish that an injury meets this legal test. The calculator assumes the applicable eligibility requirements have already been addressed and models the arithmetic for one claimant.

Family claims and claims outside Ontario auto tort

Ontario’s Family Law Act allows specified relatives to claim certain losses when someone is injured or killed through another person’s fault in circumstances covered by section 61. Clause 61(2)(e) concerns the loss of guidance, care and companionship that the claimant could reasonably have expected. Enter that relative’s own award in the family mode. A spouse’s companionship award and the injured person’s pain-and-suffering award are assessed under separate rules. Combining the two amounts could make one appear to clear a threshold that it would miss on its own. For multiple relatives, assess each person’s award separately. Enter only economic amounts recoverable by the claimant you are modelling. Ontario Family Law Act, s. 61

The Insurance Act makes an exception for damages awarded in respect of a person who dies directly or indirectly because of an incident after August 31, 2010. Select the fatal-incident checkbox for an eligible family claim so the calculation omits the deductible. The option for a slip-and-fall or another province also skips Ontario’s auto tort deduction. For an Ontario premises case there can still be a dispute about who controlled the property or whether the injured person contributed to the accident. For a claim governed by another province’s law, check the local vehicle insurance system, any applicable limits and the tax treatment of legal services before relying on a take-home estimate.

The Supreme Court of Canada’s Andrews upper limit

In 1978, the Supreme Court of Canada decided Andrews v. Grand & Toy Alberta Ltd., Thornton v. School District No. 57 (Prince George) and Arnold v. Teno. These cases established a $100,000 upper limit for non-pecuniary personal injury damages in 1978 dollars. Subsequent decisions recognize an adjustment for inflation. The Supreme Court discusses both the rationale for the ceiling and the scope of the rule in Cinar Corp. v. Robinson, citing Lindal v. Lindal for inflation adjustment. The value attributed to pain and loss of amenities depends on the facts even in a case where a court could award the indexed maximum. Andrews judgment · Cinar, paras. 94–101

This page uses about $475,000 as a rounded 2026 comparison for an individual’s personal injury pain and suffering. A CPI illustration multiplies the original $100,000 by the valuation-period Canada all-items CPI divided by the index for the 1978 reference period, using the same series and basis. For example, Statistics Canada’s unadjusted all-items index was 35.1 in January 1978 and 167.4 in March 2026, both on the 2002 = 100 basis. Dividing 167.4 by 35.1 and multiplying by $100,000 gives approximately $477,000. Using the October 2026 index of 170.4 gives approximately $485,000 by the same method. Confirm the valuation date and applicable case law for a file-specific amount. The calculator warns above the benchmark and retains the entry for comparison. It does not assign that ceiling to a relative’s separate Family Law Act claim. January 1978 CPI · March 2026 CPI · October 2026 CPI

A life-changing injury can result in damages for attendant care, equipment, home modifications or earning capacity that far exceed the amount for suffering itself. In a future-care calculation, evidence can include how frequently services are needed, which expenses a care plan supports and the present cost of meeting needs over time. The cap comparison concerns the pain-and-suffering component alone. The Ontario deductible has a different function because it reduces an award in the specified range instead of setting an upper bound on that head of damages.

How the recovery estimate is calculated

  1. Compare the gross non-pecuniary amount with the threshold for the selected claim. If the deductible applies, subtract the prescribed amount up to the value of the award. This floor prevents the deductible from creating negative damages or consuming economic recovery.
  2. Reduce both the remaining non-pecuniary amount and the combined economic amounts by the entered contributory fault percentage. Round each adjusted component to cents and add them to get gross tort recovery before fees and expenses. The breakdown also shows the total damages you entered before these adjustments.
  3. Multiply that gross recovery by the contingency percentage and round the fee to cents. Calculate and round 13% Ontario HST on that fee. Subtract the fee, its HST and your out-of-pocket disbursements. If these exceed the recovery, estimated take-home shows zero with a notice for the difference.

Income loss and care costs are outside the non-pecuniary deductible. In an Ontario auto claim, sections 267.5 and 267.8 can still govern those amounts, including the extent to which income loss before trial or health care expenses may be claimed and how qualifying collateral benefits affect recovery. Obtain the recoverable figures before entering them. If an estimate already reflects apportionment of fault, entering that fault percentage again would reduce the same amount twice. The model uses one fault assumption across the modelled heads and does not allocate liability among multiple defendants.

Thirty percent is an editable starting assumption. At this rate, the fee and HST together equal about 33.9% of the modelled fee base before rounding. A signed retainer and an itemized settlement statement can specify a different basis and treatment of costs. Interest, insurer-paid legal costs, reimbursed disbursements, advances, liens and repayment obligations can affect the final distribution. In this estimate, enter disbursements that are actually borne by the client and use the tax-inclusive amount if applicable. The 13% fee tax is a modelling assumption in all three modes. Worked examples

$150,000 plaintiff award with economic loss and shared fault

Take a $150,000 non-pecuniary award, $30,000 in recoverable income loss and $20,000 in recoverable care costs. The total entered is $200,000. The $47,913.01 deductible leaves $102,086.99 in non-pecuniary damages. At 20% contributory fault, that becomes $81,669.59, and the $50,000 in economic losses becomes $40,000.00. The combined gross tort recovery is $121,669.59. A 30% fee is $36,500.88 and HST on the fee is $4,745.11. With $2,000 in client-paid disbursements, the estimated take-home is $78,423.60. Run that example with fault set to zero to see the impact of apportionment on both heads and the fee base.

A one-dollar award change across the plaintiff threshold

A $159,708 award becomes $111,794.99 after the Ontario deduction. At $159,709 the entire award survives the deductible, a difference of $47,914.01 before any fault or fees. The exact boundary is $159,708.71 and the first amount above it at cent precision is $159,708.72. Use the four rows nearest the boundary in the scenario table to compare the dollar and cent effects with identical fee and fault assumptions. When comparing actual offers, confirm whether the figures refer to assessed general damages or to total settlement payments across several heads.

A family member’s award or an award below the deductible

For a qualifying non-fatal Ontario family claim assessed at $60,000, subtracting $23,956.52 leaves $36,043.48 before fault and fees. At $79,853.70, the family member has $55,897.18 after the deductible. At $79,853.71, the deductible vanishes. A $40,000 plaintiff award illustrates a different effect. The $47,913.01 prescribed amount absorbs all $40,000 of non-pecuniary loss, so the effective deduction shown is $40,000. A separate $10,000 in recoverable income loss would still be available before fault and fees. Comparing these cases helps identify which assumption matters more to your estimate.

Frequently asked questions

Does an award of exactly $159,708.71 escape the deductible?

No. The 2026 Ontario plaintiff award must exceed $159,708.71. At equality, the $47,913.01 deductible leaves $111,795.70 before fault and fees. At $159,708.72 the deductible is zero. For an eligible Ontario Family Law Act award, the corresponding threshold is $79,853.70 and the deductible is $23,956.52.

Can the deductible touch income loss or future care?

The non-pecuniary deductible applies only to the relevant non-pecuniary head of damages. Income loss and qualifying care costs can still be affected by liability, statutory recovery limits, and collateral benefit adjustments. Enter amounts already assessed as recoverable under those rules, before the fault percentage you enter in the calculator.

Is $475,000 the most I can recover in total?

The approximately $475,000 figure is a rounded illustration of the inflation-adjusted Andrews upper limit for personal injury pain and suffering. The applicable figure depends on the valuation date and law governing the claim. Supported care costs and lost earning capacity are separate heads. This calculator flags an individual pain-and-suffering entry over the benchmark and preserves the amount entered.

How do fault and contingency fees affect take-home recovery?

The model subtracts the Ontario deductible from non-pecuniary damages first, then reduces the remaining damages by the entered fault share. It applies the contingency percentage to that adjusted recovery, adds 13% Ontario HST to the fee, and subtracts entered client-paid disbursements. Confirm the fee base, applicable tax, and cost reimbursements with your lawyer.

Can I use the 2026 figures for a 2027 or out-of-province claim?

Confirm which jurisdiction and year apply with counsel. The calculator uses only the published 2026 Ontario figures. The 2027 statutory amounts were pending when checked on November 24, 2026. The other-claim option skips Ontario’s motor vehicle deductible. Local limits, insurance rules, and tax on legal fees can still change the result.
