What this estimate includes

The estimate assumes full-year HSA eligibility. Select the $1,000 catch-up if you are 55 or older by year-end, eligible to contribute, and unenrolled in Medicare. Contribution room is generally prorated for partial-year eligibility and is zero for every month enrolled in Medicare, including retroactive coverage. An eligible spouse makes their own catch-up contribution to a separate HSA.

For eligible Section 125 payroll contributions, the estimate uses 6.2% Social Security tax up to the annual wage base ($184,500 in 2026) and 1.45% regular Medicare tax. A deduction that moves wages below the wage base can save Social Security tax on that portion. This estimate excludes Additional Medicare Tax. Direct contributions generally provide no payroll tax savings. See the SSA wage base and IRS payroll tax rates.

Use 0% for state contribution savings in California and New Jersey, where state treatment differs from federal HSA rules. The growth and withdrawal figures reflect federal tax treatment.

The projection adds the same eligible employee and employer amounts at the end of each year and compounds at a constant annual return. It assumes no fees, withdrawals, or future changes to contributions or eligibility.

FAQ

What are the official 2026 IRS HSA contribution limits?

The 2026 limits are $4,400 for self-only coverage and $8,750 for family coverage. An eligible account holder age 55 or older by year-end may add $1,000. Employer contributions count toward the limit.

What happens if HSA funds are spent on non-qualified expenses?

Non-qualified withdrawals are generally subject to ordinary federal income tax. An additional 20% federal tax generally applies before age 65, with exceptions for disability or death. At age 65 or older, ordinary income tax still applies to non-qualified withdrawals. Qualified medical withdrawals are federally tax-free.

See IRS Publication 969 for eligibility and distribution rules.
