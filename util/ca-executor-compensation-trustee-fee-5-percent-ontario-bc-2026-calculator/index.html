How Canadian Estate Executor & Trustee Compensation Works in 2026

Serving as an estate trustee (Ontario) or executor (British Columbia and other common-law provinces) carries substantial legal, accounting, and personal liability responsibilities—ranging from filing the terminal T1 income tax return and T3 Trust return to securing a CRA Clearance Certificate before distributing estate assets. When a Will is silent on remuneration, provincial trustee statutes grant executors the right to claim a "fair and reasonable" allowance for their care, pains, trouble, and time. However, how courts calculate that allowance differs sharply between Ontario and British Columbia—and for family executors who are also beneficiaries, claiming an executor fee can accidentally trigger a massive Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) tax loss.

Ontario's 2.5% Case-Law Tariff vs. British Columbia's Statutory 5% Cap

Although both provinces apply judicial discretion based on estate complexity, practitioners start with two distinct provincial benchmarks:

  • Ontario Tariff (Trustee Act s.61 & Toronto General Trusts v. Central Ontario Railway): Ontario courts begin with a percentage guideline of 2.5% on capital receipts, 2.5% on capital disbursements, 2.5% on revenue receipts, and 2.5% on revenue disbursements—effectively totaling 5% of fully administered capital and 5% of estate income—plus an annual 0.4% (2/5 of 1%) care and management fee calculated on the average value of ongoing trust assets. Importantly, Ontario's tariff is a starting benchmark, not a guaranteed floor or ceiling: courts routinely reduce compensation on large, simple liquid estates (such as a single C$2 million home and one brokerage account) and may award extra compensation on unusually litigious or multi-jurisdictional estates.
  • British Columbia Cap (Trustee Act, RSBC 1996, c. 464, s.88): Unlike Ontario, British Columbia imposes a strict statutory maximum of 5% of the gross aggregate value (including both capital and income) of the estate, plus an annual care and management fee of up to 0.4% per year on the average market value of trust assets. Because 5% is reserved for the most complex, time-intensive estates, BC Supreme Court registrars frequently award between 2.5% and 4.0% on routine estates.

In both provinces, courts cross-check any percentage figure against the five classic fairness factors established in Canadian case law: (1) the magnitude of the estate, (2) the care and responsibility involved, (3) the actual time occupied by the executor, (4) the skill and ability displayed, and (5) the success achieved in administering and preserving estate value. Keeping a contemporaneous time log of every hour spent dealing with lawyers, banks, accountants, realtors, and CRA is the single strongest piece of evidence if a beneficiary challenges your passing of accounts.

The Sole-Beneficiary & Sibling Tax Trap: Taxable Income vs. Tax-Free Bequest

Canada has no federal inheritance tax: money received as a beneficiary under a Will arrives 100% tax-free (the estate itself pays any terminal capital gains or RRSP deemed-disposition tax before distribution). By sharp contrast, executor compensation is fully taxable personal income under the Income Tax Act. Even more importantly, the CRA classifies a non-professional family executor's fee as income from an "office" (analogous to employment income), meaning the estate must register a CRA payroll account, issue a T4 slip, withhold personal income tax, and remit both the employee and employer portions of Canada Pension Plan (CPP) contributions (up to the 2026 maximum annual pensionable threshold unless the executor is already maxed out at work or over age 70).

Consider what happens when a family executor who sits in a 43.41% marginal tax bracket is also a 50% or 100% beneficiary of the estate:

  • If You Are the Sole Beneficiary (100% Share): Claiming a C$67,000 executor fee is pure financial self-sabotage. You pay C$29,085 in income tax plus C$3,987 in employee CPP (not counting the estate's employer CPP share), netting C$33,928—while reducing your own tax-free inheritance by the exact same C$67,000 (because the estate generally cannot deduct personal capital administration fees on the T3 return, except narrow investment-income-earning expenses). You literally donate over C$33,000 to the CRA for nothing!
  • If You Are One of Two Equal Siblings (50% Share): On a C$67,000 fee at a 43.41% tax rate plus CPP, your after-tax net cash is C$33,928, while your own 50% share of the tax-free residue drops by C$33,500—leaving a tiny net incremental gain of just C$428 in exchange for payroll filings and potential sibling resentment!
  • If You Are One of Four Siblings (25% Share) or a Non-Beneficiary (0% Share): Here claiming a fee becomes strongly net positive, because 75% to 100% of the gross fee comes from other beneficiaries' shares rather than your own tax-free residue.

When Does an Executor Fee Reduce Estate Tax? (Capital vs. Income Expenses)

A common misconception is that the estate gets a full tax deduction whenever it pays an executor fee. Under CRA administrative policy, only the portion of executor compensation directly attributable to managing income-producing property (for example, collecting dividends, interest, or rental income during estate administration) is deductible on the estate's T3 Trust Income Tax Return. The much larger portion tied to gathering capital assets, selling the family residence, paying debts, and distributing capital legacies is a non-deductible capital expense. As a result, paying an executor fee usually creates one-sided taxation: fully taxed in the executor's hands without a matching tax shield for the estate.

Practical Checklist Before Taking or Waiving Executor Compensation

  1. Read the Will's Compensation Clause First: Some Wills fix compensation at a specific dollar sum, cap it at 2%–3%, or state that a legacy is given "in lieu of executor compensation" (which creates a legal presumption that you cannot claim both the gift and a statutory fee unless you renounce the gift).
  2. Never Pre-Take Compensation Without Consent or Court Order: In Ontario and BC, taking an advance on executor compensation before final passing of accounts—without either express authorization in the Will or unanimous written consent from all adult, legally capable residual beneficiaries—is a breach of fiduciary duty that can trigger interest surcharges and court removal.
  3. Check Whether Co-Executors Split a Single Tariff: Appointing two or three siblings as co-executors does not double or triple the tariff; co-executors share a single global compensation pool divided in proportion to the work each performed.
  4. Document Professional Fees Paid by the Estate: If the estate hires an accountant or estate lawyer to perform tasks the executor would ordinarily handle personally (such as preparing estate accounts rather than providing legal advice), courts frequently deduct those overlapping professional bills directly from the executor's compensation award.

Worked 2026 Ontario vs. British Columbia Estate Comparison

Consider a common C$1.2 million Canadian estate consisting of a family home and investment portfolio, plus C$60,000 in post-death dividends and interest earned during a one-year administration (average trust value C$1.0 million). Under Ontario's 2.5% tariff, capital receipts and disbursements generate (C$1,200,000 + C$1,200,000) × 2.5% = C$60,000, revenue flows generate (C$60,000 + C$60,000) × 2.5% = C$3,000, and one year of care and management adds C$1,000,000 × 0.4% = C$4,000—producing a guideline total of C$67,000. Under British Columbia's Trustee Act s.88, the strict statutory 5% ceiling on gross aggregate value (C$1,260,000) is C$63,000 plus care and management; at a typical court-approved mid-range award of 3.5%, BC compensation equals C$44,100 + C$4,000 = C$48,100.

Now run the tax trade-off for an adult child serving as executor in a 43.41% marginal tax bracket who has not yet maxed out CPP contributions. If the executor is a 50% residual beneficiary (splitting the estate equally with one sibling) and claims the C$67,000 Ontario fee, personal income tax takes C$29,085 and employee CPP takes C$3,987—leaving C$33,928 net cash. Yet paying that C$67,000 fee out of the estate reduces the executor's own 50% tax-free inheritance share by C$33,500, shrinking the real economic gain to just C$428 while handing more than C$33,000 to CRA (plus employer CPP!). If that same executor owns a 60% or 100% beneficiary share, claiming the fee triggers a steep guaranteed net loss. Always model both the provincial guideline and the personal marginal-tax crossover before deciding whether to claim or waive executor remuneration.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is executor compensation calculated in Ontario vs British Columbia in 2026?

Ontario courts use a case-law guideline tariff of 2.5% on capital receipts, 2.5% on capital disbursements, 2.5% on revenue receipts, and 2.5% on revenue disbursements (roughly 5% overall), plus 0.4% per year for ongoing care and management. British Columbia's Trustee Act s.88 enforces a strict statutory cap of 5% of gross aggregate value plus up to 0.4% per year for care and management.

Is an executor fee taxable in Canada?

Yes. Unlike an inheritance bequest—which is received tax-free in Canada—executor compensation is fully taxable as income from an office (for non-professional executors) or business income (for professional trustees). The estate must generally issue a T4 slip and remit income tax and CPP deductions.

Should a family executor who is also a major beneficiary waive their executor fee?

Often yes. If you are the sole beneficiary (100% share), claiming an executor fee simply converts your own tax-free inheritance into high-bracket taxable income plus CPP. Even with a 50% beneficiary share, high marginal tax rates (43%–53%) can wipe out virtually all net benefit once you account for the reduced tax-free residue.

Can an executor take their fee before all beneficiaries agree?

No. Unless the Will explicitly authorizes pre-taking compensation, an executor must obtain unanimous written consent from all competent residual beneficiaries (via a signed Release and Approval of Accounts) or secure a formal court order on a Passing of Accounts before paying themselves.
