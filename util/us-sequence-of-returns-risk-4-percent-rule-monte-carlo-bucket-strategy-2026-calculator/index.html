Loss aversion can make a bad year worse

Selling stocks to cover spending after a decline leaves fewer shares to participate in a recovery. A cash buffer can postpone some sales, but cash has an opportunity cost and can run out. Guardrails preserve more capital by reducing spending, so check whether the cuts fit your essential expenses.

How the simulation works

  • Market path. The historical presets use three published annual S&P 500 total returns from the named episode. Later returns are synthetic and calibrated to a 7% geometric mean. The default custom path has three losses, 13 years at 5%, and 14 years of roughly 14.95%. The bull-first comparison reverses the entire return list and keeps the same inflation and withdrawal timing.
  • Fixed 4% rule. Each year, returns are applied first and spending is withdrawn at year-end. After year one, the planned dollar withdrawal grows with the inflation input, even in a loss year. A depletion warning marks when assets reach zero, including a year when the last withdrawal was fully funded.
  • Guyton–Klinger-inspired guardrails. During a negative stock year, take the chosen cut if the pre-withdrawal balance is more than 15% below the inflation-adjusted starting balance. The reduced amount becomes the base for later inflation adjustments. The original Guyton–Klinger method uses different thresholds and additional rules.
  • Cash bucket. Set aside part of the starting balance in a modeled T-bill or money market reserve earning 4.0% annually. Use cash first in negative equity years. Otherwise spend from equity first, then refill the reserve toward the chosen years of next year's spending after a positive return when another year remains.

The model uses constant user-entered inflation, including for the 1973 preset. Results are before taxes and fees and exclude Social Security, pensions, required minimum distributions and other cash flows. Each result represents one path. The maximum fixed withdrawal rate is a mathematical break-even for that path and gives no probability of success.

Common questions

Why can two retirees earn 7% annualized and finish with different balances?

A compound average describes growth without withdrawals. Early withdrawals after losses leave fewer dollars invested in the later recovery. The two fixed strategies use the same returns in opposite order, so their different balances come from the timing of returns and withdrawals.

How does the 4% rule handle inflation?

It starts with 4% of the original portfolio. Later withdrawals adjust the prior year's dollar amount for inflation. The percentage of the remaining portfolio can therefore rise when markets fall.

When does the simulator cut spending under its guardrail?

It cuts planned spending in a negative stock year when the portfolio, after the return and before the withdrawal, is more than 15% below its inflation-adjusted starting value. That cut carries forward into future planned spending. The original Guyton–Klinger method uses additional rules.

Does a three-year cash bucket prevent a retirement shortfall?

A cash reserve can cover spending in down years until it runs out. It starts inside your existing portfolio and earns the modeled 4% cash rate, so holding cash also changes your exposure to a rebound.

What does the maximum initial withdrawal rate mean?

It is the highest initial fixed withdrawal rate that funds every inflation-adjusted withdrawal on the selected bear-first path. The final withdrawal uses the last of the portfolio. Different returns, higher costs or a longer retirement can change the result.
