Key takeaways

  • Canada’s general capital gains inclusion rate is 50% in 2026. The proposed 66.67% rate for individual gains above $250,000 and for corporations and trusts was cancelled in March 2025. The general 50% rate applies to all three taxpayer types. This calculator uses individual tax brackets.
  • The indexed 2026 LCGE ceiling is $1,275,000. The $1,250,000 base was indexed by 2% in 2026. Prior claims reduce the room available on eligible small-business shares and qualifying farm or fishing property.
  • The taxable gain stacks on top of other income. Each slice is taxed at its federal and provincial bracket rate.

Frequently asked questions

Does the 66.67% inclusion rate apply above $250,000 in 2026?

The government cancelled the proposed increase on March 21, 2025. Ordinary capital gains for individuals, corporations and trusts continue to use the general 50% inclusion rate. Special statutory rules can apply to particular dispositions.

How much is the lifetime capital gains exemption in 2026?

The indexed LCGE ceiling is $1,275,000 of qualifying gains for 2026. It can apply to qualified small business corporation shares and qualified farm or fishing property. Prior claims and other deduction limits can reduce the amount available. Calculate the claim using CRA Form T657.

How do I enter capital losses and previous LCGE claims?

Enter each amount on a gross-gain basis. For example, $5,000 of allowable loss at a 50% inclusion rate is $10,000 in the loss field. Adjust carryforwards for any difference in inclusion rates before entering them. Enter only losses available to offset this gain.

Why does the same capital gain cost different amounts across provinces?

Each province has its own income brackets. The calculator stacks the taxable gain on top of your other taxable income and taxes each slice at its corresponding rate. For Quebec it also applies the 16.5% federal abatement to the added federal bracket tax.

Can my spouse report part of the capital gain?

Use your actual reportable share based on ownership and attribution rules. Transferring property to a spouse can leave the gain attributable to you. The comparison assumes both owners live in the same province and neither applies personal capital losses or the LCGE.
