Compare RRSP, TFSA, FHSA, RESP and taxable accounts. Calculate the dividend tax drag and see when switching from VFV to VOO in an RRSP could pay for the currency conversion.

Key takeaways

A U.S.-listed ETF held directly in an eligible RRSP generally incurs 0% U.S. withholding on qualifying U.S. dividends. A Canadian-listed wrapper such as VFV or XUS generally incurs 15% at the fund level. At a 1.5% U.S. dividend yield, that costs 0.225% of portfolio value in year one. TFSA, FHSA and RESP holdings generally face the same 15% withholding. Taxable accounts may qualify for a foreign tax credit.

How the estimate works

Multiply gross U.S. dividend yield by 15% to get annual withholding as a share of the portfolio. Reduce this drag by any assumed credit. Wealth lost is the difference between compounding the gross return for 25 years and compounding that return minus the net drag.

For the FX comparison, the conversion fee comes out of the starting balance. The remaining balance compounds at the gross return. The Canadian wrapper compounds at the same return less its withholding drag. Break-even solves for when both balances are equal. The 20-year comparison includes the growth forgone on the fee.

The model assumes one starting investment, constant yearly rates and reinvestment of dividends and any assumed credit at year-end. It isolates U.S. withholding and one-way FX costs. Fund fees, Canadian income taxes, RRSP withdrawal taxes, return conversion costs and exchange-rate movements can affect the comparison.

For international equity ETFs, taxes charged by the underlying countries may apply before the U.S. or Canadian fund receives the dividend. A U.S.-listed fund held in an RRSP may still incur that underlying withholding. Use this model for the U.S. withholding layer and check the fund structure separately. Frequently asked questions

Why does VOO generally avoid U.S. dividend withholding in an RRSP while VFV incurs it?

Article XXI(2) of the Canada–U.S. tax treaty generally exempts qualifying U.S. dividends received directly by an eligible RRSP. With VFV or XUS, the Canadian fund receives the U.S. dividends and generally incurs withholding before the money reaches your RRSP.

Does buying a U.S.-listed ETF avoid withholding in a TFSA, FHSA or RESP?

Qualifying U.S. dividends generally face 15% withholding in these accounts under either wrapper. These accounts fall outside the treaty retirement exemption used in this comparison, and the modeled withholding is unrecoverable in the account.

Can I recover the full 15% in a non-registered account?

Full recovery is possible if all withholding qualifies and the applicable Canadian tax limit covers it. For the federal credit, use CRA Form T2209. A Canadian ETF may designate eligible amounts on a T3 slip. A provincial or territorial credit may also apply.

When does Norbert’s Gambit pay for itself?

Break-even depends on the amount invested, eligible dividend yield, return and total conversion cost. At C$100,000, 1.5% gross U.S. dividend yield and 7% total return, a C$20 Gambit breaks even in about 0.10 year. A 1.5% retail FX fee takes about 7.18 years.

Does this estimate cover all withholding on international ETFs?

It covers the U.S. withholding layer. The countries that issued the underlying stocks may withhold their own tax before the dividend reaches the fund. Those amounts depend on the fund structure, source country and treaties, and can still apply when an RRSP receives a distribution free of U.S. withholding.
