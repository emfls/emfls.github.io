How the estimate works

The 2026 §415(c) base ceiling is the lesser of $72,000 or 100% of applicable compensation. Subtract regular elective deferrals up to $24,500 and all employer additions including any allocated forfeitures. A percentage-based employer estimate uses at most $360,000 of eligible annual pay.

Eligible catch-up contributions sit above the annual additions limit. The age-based amounts are $8,000 from age 50 and $11,250 at ages 60–63, for statutory totals of $80,000 and $83,250 respectively. For example, $24,500 in regular deferrals and $10,000 in employer additions leave $37,500 of base room at sufficient compensation.

The estimate covers one standard plan before any voluntary after-tax contributions already made. Subtract those separately. A plan may cap or refund after-tax contributions because of payroll, matching or nondiscrimination rules. Related-employer plans can share limits. Verify plan-specific compensation, available room and conversion rules before contributing.

Frequently asked questions

Which 401(k) features do I need?

The plan needs voluntary after-tax employee contributions plus either an in-plan Roth conversion or an eligible in-service distribution that can be rolled into a Roth IRA. Confirm that the chosen feature covers your after-tax source.

Does my catch-up contribution reduce the after-tax room?

A qualifying catch-up under §414(v) is outside the annual additions limit. The calculator shows age-based ceilings separately. Payroll determines which deferrals qualify, including whether the 2026 Roth catch-up rule applies.

Can I owe tax when I convert?

After-tax contribution basis generally carries no additional federal income tax at conversion. Pre-tax earnings moved into Roth can be taxable. Ask your plan how it handles earnings and distribution allocation.
