Complete Guide to Australian Workers' Compensation Weekly Payments

Suffering a workplace injury in Australia initiates a statutory claims process under state-specific workers' compensation legislation—most prominently the Workers Compensation Act 1987 (NSW) administered by the State Insurance Regulatory Authority (SIRA), and the Workplace Injury Rehabilitation and Compensation Act 2013 (VIC) administered by WorkSafe Victoria. A core objective of the statutory scheme is replacing lost wages through structured weekly income payments based on the injured worker's Pre-Injury Average Weekly Earnings (PIAWE).

1. Calculating Pre-Injury Average Weekly Earnings (PIAWE)

PIAWE represents the foundational baseline against which all statutory compensation calculations are benchmarked. It is calculated as the average gross weekly earnings of the worker during the 52 weeks immediately preceding the date of injury. The calculation components include:

  • Base Earnings: Normal weekly salary or hourly wages paid for contracted ordinary hours of work.
  • Overtime Payments: Overtime worked on a regular, systematic basis is explicitly included in PIAWE for the first 52 weeks of payments under NSW legislation.
  • Shift Allowances: Regular shift allowances, penalty rates, and site allowances that form an integral part of weekly remuneration.
  • Excluded Non-Cash Benefits: Employer superannuation guarantee contributions (SGC) and non-cash fringe benefits (such as private use of a company vehicle) are generally excluded from monetary PIAWE calculations unless specifically negotiated under industrial instruments.

2. SIRA Statutory Maximum Weekly Compensation Cap ($2,662.10/wk)

Regardless of an executive's or specialist trade's high pre-injury salary, weekly payments are subject to a strict statutory maximum cap under Section 34 of the NSW Workers Compensation Act 1987. This cap is indexed twice annually (on 1 April and 1 October) in line with Average Weekly Earnings. For the 2026–2027 statutory period (1 April 2026 to 31 March 2027), the NSW maximum statutory cap is $2,662.10 per week. Victoria WorkSafe maintains an equivalent statutory cap pegged to twice Victorian Average Weekly Earnings (indexed to approximately $2,740.00/week).

3. The Statutory Entitlement Periods & Step-Down System

Australian compensation schemes are deliberately structured with financial incentives designed to encourage early rehabilitation and gradual return to work. Benefits follow three distinct statutory entitlement periods:

First Entitlement Period (Weeks 1 to 13)

During the initial acute recovery phase (the first 13 weeks of compensation):

  • Total Incapacity (No Current Work Capacity): The worker receives 95% of PIAWE, subject to the statutory maximum cap ($2,662.10/wk).
  • Partial Return to Work (Current Work Capacity): The worker receives 95% of PIAWE minus their actual gross weekly earnings (E). This formula ensures the worker is financially rewarded for returning to suitable duties.

Second Entitlement Period (Weeks 14 to 130)

Commencing at Week 14 and extending to the milestone of 130 weeks (approximately 2.5 years post-injury), statutory payments step down to reduce ongoing scheme liabilities:

  • Total Incapacity (No Work Capacity): The statutory benefit rate steps down to 80% of PIAWE (capped at statutory maximum).
  • Return-to-Work Incentive Boost (NSW): In New South Wales, workers who demonstrate an active return to suitable employment by working 15 hours or more per week and earning at least $210 per week receive a statutory incentive boost: their compensation rate is elevated back to 95% of PIAWE minus current earnings, rather than the lower 80% rate.
  • Low-Hour Return to Work (<15 hrs/week): Workers working fewer than 15 hours receive 80% of PIAWE minus current earnings.

4. The 130-Week "Cliff" & Whole Person Impairment (WPI) Thresholds

The 130-week milestone is known in Australian personal injury law as the statutory "cliff." Under Section 38 of the NSW Act and Section 165 of the Victorian Act, weekly income payments strictly cease at 130 weeks for the vast majority of injured workers. Payments will continue beyond 130 weeks only if one of two narrow statutory exceptions is established:

  1. Serious Injury / High Needs Threshold: The worker has undergone a formal American Medical Association (AMA) 5th Edition impairment assessment by an accredited medical specialist resulting in a Whole Person Impairment (WPI) of 21% or greater (in Victoria, "serious injury" status with 21%+ WPI or equivalent pain and suffering certificates).
  2. Permanent Incapacity with Substantial Employment: The worker has returned to suitable employment for at least 15 hours per week earning at least $210/week, but medical evidence definitively establishes that they are permanently incapable of further increasing their hours.

Workers who do not satisfy the 21%+ WPI threshold transition off weekly statutory payments, relying on private income protection, Medicare, Centrelink Disability Support Pensions (DSP), or pursuing common law work injury damages (negligence claims) if employer breach of duty can be established.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

1. Are workers' compensation weekly payments taxable in Australia?
Yes. Weekly compensation payments are classified by the Australian Taxation Office (ATO) as ordinary assessable income because they replace lost wages. Your insurer or employer will deduct Pay As You Go (PAYG) withholding tax before depositing your weekly payments. However, lump-sum permanent impairment compensations (WPI payouts) are generally exempt from income tax.
2. Does superannuation continue to be paid while on workers' compensation?
Under modern award rules and the National Employment Standards (NES), employers in Australia are generally not required to pay the Superannuation Guarantee Contribution (SGC) on workers' compensation weekly payments unless an enterprise agreement (EBA) or individual employment contract explicitly mandates it.
3. Can my employer terminate my employment while on workers' comp?
Under Section 248 of the Workers Compensation Act 1987 (NSW), employers are strictly prohibited from dismissing an injured worker within 6 months of injury solely because the worker is unfit to work. After 6 months, an employer may terminate employment only if they have fulfilled all statutory return-to-work obligations and no suitable alternative duties can reasonably be provided.
4. What is the difference between statutory benefits and a common law claim?
Statutory weekly payments are "no-fault" benefits provided automatically regardless of who caused the incident. A common law Work Injury Damages claim requires proving that the employer’s negligence caused the injury, and in NSW requires a threshold of at least 15% Whole Person Impairment.
