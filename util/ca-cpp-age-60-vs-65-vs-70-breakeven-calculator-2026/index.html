Starting sooner brings cash when you may need it, while delaying buys a larger inflation-adjusted payment for as long as you live. Consider how you would cover expenses during the wait and what a longer retirement would cost. CPP survivor benefits follow separate eligibility and combination rules. How the comparison works

The age-65 estimate is held constant across claiming scenarios in age-60 purchasing power. The model uses monthly payments, with the first arriving one month after the selected start age. It grows the common base with the monthly equivalent of your annual CPI assumption, including the years before claiming. Actual CPP payments in pay are indexed each January, and future starting entitlements can change with earnings history and benefit rules.

Nominal totals add the projected cheques after tax. Real totals convert them to age-60 purchasing power. Present value discounts each after-tax cheque back to age 60. The effective real rate is (1 + nominal return) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1. The break-even age is the first payment month when the later claim has caught up on the selected basis. A sufficiently high opportunity cost can prevent a crossover entirely.

This estimate excludes future contribution changes to your pension, post-retirement benefits, survivor benefits, OAS/GIS interactions and tax changes across retirement. Calculations run in your browser.

Frequently asked questions

How does starting CPP at 60 or 70 change the monthly pension?

Before 65, CPP falls by 0.6% for each month you start early. After 65, it rises by 0.7% per month up to age 70. With the same age-65 base, age 60 pays 64% and age 70 pays 142%, about 2.22 times the age-60 amount.

At what age does delayed CPP break even?

With no growth, discounting or tax, age 65 catches age 60 at 73 years 11 months, age 70 catches age 65 at 81 years 11 months, and age 70 catches age 60 at 78 years 3 months. These are the first month-ends at which the later claim has paid at least as much. Inflation and the opportunity cost of waiting can change the crossover.

How do inflation and income tax affect the comparison?

Service Canada adjusts CPP benefits in pay each January using CPI. The calculator models a steady monthly CPI equivalent and applies the same marginal tax rate to every payment. That common tax rate scales the totals equally, so it leaves crossover ages unchanged as long as there is after-tax income.

How much CPP2 will I pay in 2026?

CPP2 applies to pensionable earnings above $74,600 up to $85,000. Employees and employers each pay 4%, up to $416 each. Self-employed contributors pay 8%, up to $832. The $3,500 exemption applies to the first contribution tier.

What happens if I die before breaking even?

Your own retirement pension ends at death. An eligible spouse or common-law partner may receive a separate survivor’s pension. A survivor’s own CPP can affect the combined amount. The comparison assumes you live to each age shown and excludes any survivor payment.
