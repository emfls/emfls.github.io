How the estimate works

Add cash, spendable brokerage proceeds and available regular Roth contribution basis, then subtract inflation-adjusted spending for 2026 to 2030. Each annual conversion target equals projected spending in its unlock year and is assumed fully taxable at conversion.

Incremental 2026 tax is the difference between federal ordinary income tax with and without the conversion. The base deduction is $16,100 single, $32,200 jointly, or $24,150 head of household. Bracket room shows amounts within each band after the proposed 2026 conversion.

The penalty comparison assumes a direct taxable traditional IRA or 401(k) withdrawal for the same gross amount at the scheduled unlock date with no exception. The optional withdrawal scenario applies the Roth ordering rules to the specific balances you enter.

Scope and assumptions

The federal estimate uses ordinary-income brackets and the base standard deduction. It excludes tax-table rounding, other deductions and credits, state taxes, ACA subsidies, Medicare, investment gains and statutory exceptions to the 10% tax. The bridge assumes no account growth and excludes conversion tax, which needs separate funding. Future tax brackets, Roth growth and net retirement savings are outside the projection.

Frequently asked questions

When does a 2026 Roth conversion clear its five-tax-year clock?

The clock begins January 1, 2026 and ends January 1, 2031. Each conversion year has its own five-tax-year period. Age 59½ and certain other exceptions can remove the 10% additional tax earlier.

What comes out first from a Roth IRA?

Regular contributions come out first, followed by conversions and rollovers in tax-year order. Within a year, previously taxable conversion principal comes out before nontaxable conversion principal. Earnings come out last.

Does the conversion clock make Roth earnings tax-free?

Qualified earnings distributions generally require the separate Roth IRA five-tax-year rule and a qualifying condition such as age 59½. If earnings are nonqualified, they can be taxable and can incur a 10% additional tax before age 59½ absent an exception.
