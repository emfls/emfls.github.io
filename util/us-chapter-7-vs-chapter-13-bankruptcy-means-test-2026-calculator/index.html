Comprehensive Guide to the 2026 US Bankruptcy Means Test

Filing for bankruptcy under the United States Bankruptcy Code (Title 11) is one of the most consequential legal and financial decisions an individual or married couple can make. Established under the Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act (BAPCPA) of 2005, the Bankruptcy Means Test acts as the statutory gatekeeper determining whether a consumer debtor is eligible to eliminate qualifying debts entirely under Chapter 7 liquidation, or whether they must instead commit their projected disposable income into a court-supervised 3-to-5-year repayment plan under Chapter 13 reorganization.

Step 1: The Median Income Comparison (Official Form 122A-1)

The first step of the Means Test is designed as a broad administrative screen. Under 11 U.S.C. § 707(b)(7), debtors whose aggregate household income falls at or below the statistical median income for their state and household size qualify for a complete statutory Safe Harbor. If you qualify for the Safe Harbor, you are legally excused from completing the complex expense-deduction worksheets of Form 122A-2; the presumption of abuse does not arise, and you are immediately eligible for Chapter 7 relief.

Calculating Current Monthly Income (CMI) requires compiling all gross income received from every source during the 6 full calendar months preceding the petition date, dividing by six, and multiplying by 12 to establish annualized income. Key statutory nuances include:

  • Gross Income vs. Take-Home Pay: CMI is calculated using gross income prior to taxes, 401(k) retirement contributions, or medical premiums.
  • Exclusion of Social Security: Pursuant to 11 U.S.C. § 101(10A), Social Security Act payments (retirement, survivor, disability, and SSI benefits) are strictly excluded from CMI.
  • Non-Debtor Spouse Contributions: If filing individually, income contributed on a regular basis to household expenses by a non-filing spouse must be disclosed, though separate expenses may be deducted via the marital adjustment.

Step 2: Allowable Expense Deductions & Presumption of Abuse (Form 122A-2)

If your annualized CMI exceeds your state’s median income benchmark, you must complete the second phase of the Means Test under 11 U.S.C. § 707(b)(2). The statute replaces actual consumer expenditures with standardized living expense allowances published by the Internal Revenue Service (IRS), preventing high-income debtors from claiming lavish lifestyle costs to justify liquidation:

  • IRS National Standards: Fixed standardized allowances for food, housekeeping supplies, apparel and services, personal care products, and miscellaneous expenses based on family size.
  • IRS Local Standards: County-specific housing and utilities allowances, plus regional vehicle operating and nationwide vehicle ownership allowances.
  • Other Necessary Expenses: Actual expenses for mandatory payroll taxes (FICA/Medicare), court-ordered child support, mandatory retirement contributions, term life insurance, and actual health insurance premiums.
  • Contractual Secured Debt Deductions: Average monthly payments due on secured debts (such as home mortgages and motor vehicle financing) scheduled over the next 60 months, plus any past-due cure amounts divided by 60.

The 60-Month Disposable Income Thresholds

Subtracting allowable standardized and actual expenses from CMI yields the debtor's Monthly Disposable Income (MDI). This figure is multiplied by 60 to project five years of debt-paying capability. The statute establishes three distinct outcome tiers:

  1. Under $10,000 ($166.67/month): The presumption of abuse does NOT arise. The debtor passes the Means Test and is fully eligible to proceed under Chapter 7 liquidation.
  2. Over $16,000 ($266.67/month): A statutory presumption of abuse arises automatically. The bankruptcy court presumes that filing Chapter 7 is an abuse of the bankruptcy system. Unless the debtor can establish "special circumstances" (such as a serious medical condition or active-duty military call-up) with no reasonable alternative, the case will be dismissed or converted to Chapter 13.
  3. Between $10,000 and $16,000: The court applies a secondary ratio test. If the 60-month disposable income is sufficient to pay at least 25% of the debtor's nonpriority unsecured claims, the presumption of abuse arises. If it pays less than 25%, the debtor passes the Means Test.

Chapter 7 vs Chapter 13: Strategic Tradeoffs

While Chapter 7 offers immediate relief—typically granting a complete discharge of unsecured credit cards, personal loans, and medical bills within 3 to 4 months without making creditor payments—it does not provide tools to halt home foreclosures permanently if you are substantially behind on mortgage arrears. In contrast, Chapter 13 reorganization allows debtors to cure mortgage defaults over a 36-to-60-month repayment plan, strip wholly unsecured second mortgages, and protect non-exempt assets from trustee liquidation.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

1. Can I file Chapter 7 if my income is over the state median?
Yes. Being above the state median simply means you must complete Form 122A-2. If your allowable IRS deductions, secured mortgage payments, and tax obligations reduce your 60-month disposable income below statutory thresholds, you successfully rebut the presumption of abuse and qualify for Chapter 7.
2. Does the Means Test apply to business bankruptcy or non-consumer debt?
No. Under 11 U.S.C. § 707(b)(1), the Means Test applies only to individual debtors whose debts are primarily consumer debts (debts incurred for personal, family, or household purposes). If more than 50% of your total liabilities represent business debts, tax obligations, or commercial guarantees, you are exempt from the Means Test.
3. What qualifies as "Special Circumstances" to rebut a presumption of abuse?
Under 11 U.S.C. § 707(b)(2)(B), special circumstances include catastrophic medical emergencies, prolonged unemployment, or call-up to active military duty where there is no reasonable alternative expenditure. Debtors must provide extensive documentation and sworn itemized justifications.
4. How often are state median income figures updated?
The United States Trustee Program (USTP) updates state median family income data biannually (typically in April and November) based on Census Bureau compilations. This calculator reflects the verified 2026 statutory figures.
