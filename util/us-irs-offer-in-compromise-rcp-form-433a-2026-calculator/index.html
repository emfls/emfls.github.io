Understanding the IRS Offer in Compromise Formula (Internal Revenue Manual 5.8)

An Offer in Compromise (OIC) under Internal Revenue Code § 7122 allows qualifying taxpayers facing severe economic distress to resolve their outstanding federal tax debt for less than the full amount owed. The IRS evaluates every submission using an exact statutory metric termed Reasonable Collection Potential (RCP).

1. The Two Pillars of Reasonable Collection Potential (RCP)

The IRS does not negotiate arbitrarily; the RCP represents the mathematical baseline below which the IRS cannot legally accept an offer under the "Doubt as to Collectibility" standard:

  • Net Realizable Equity in Assets: Calculated as the Quick Sale Value (QSV) of physical and financial property minus senior encumbrances. The IRS Internal Revenue Manual prescribes an 80% discount factor on the fair market value of real estate, personal vehicles, and equipment to reflect forced-liquidation speed. Cash, bank accounts, and certificates of deposit are evaluated at 100% of liquid face value.
  • Future Remaining Income: The difference between your verified monthly gross income and the IRS Collection Financial Standards (national and local allowable caps on food, clothing, housing, utilities, vehicle operational costs, and healthcare).

2. Lump Sum Cash Offer vs Periodic Payment Offer

Under IRS Form 656 guidelines, taxpayers must select one of two statutory payment tracks:

  • Lump Sum Cash Offer: You must submit a non-refundable 20% down payment with your Form 656 package. Upon formal acceptance, the remaining 80% balance must be paid in 5 or fewer payments within 5 calendar months. The future income multiplier is capped at 12 months, making this the cheapest settlement method.
  • Periodic Payment Offer: You must submit your first proposed monthly installment with the initial application and continue making monthly payments while the IRS examines the case (which typically takes 9 to 18 months). The future income multiplier is expanded to 24 months, resulting in a substantially higher settlement total.

3. The 10-Year CSED Rule (Collection Statute Expiration Date)

Under IRC § 6502, the IRS has exactly 10 years (120 months) from the date of tax assessment to collect unpaid taxes. If your monthly disposable income is large enough to full-pay the outstanding tax debt via an installment agreement before the CSED expires, the IRS will conclude that you have the "Ability to Pay" and will issue a formal rejection of your Form 656 offer.
