2026 NUA Calculator

Compare taking 401(k) company stock in kind under the net unrealized appreciation (NUA) rules with a direct rollover to a traditional IRA.

Key takeaways

  • NUA can lower federal tax when a large share of the stock’s value is built-in gain. The taxable stock basis generates ordinary income in the distribution year, and a 10% early-distribution tax may apply.
  • Realized built-in NUA qualifies for long-term capital gains rates and stays exempt from the 3.8% net investment income tax (NIIT) when sold. Gains above the distribution value follow the usual holding-period and NIIT rules.
  • A direct rollover of eligible pretax funds to a traditional IRA defers federal income tax. Later withdrawals from the all-pretax balance are ordinary income.

What you could lose

Rolling the company shares into an IRA generally forfeits NUA treatment for those shares. Confirm the plan can send shares in kind to a taxable brokerage account and direct-roll the eligible remaining balance before authorizing either transfer.

A price drop can shrink or erase the gain eligible for NUA treatment after you have paid the upfront tax. Try a negative growth rate to see how a decline affects the comparison, especially when employer stock makes up a large share of your retirement savings.

NUA eligibility checklist

  1. Confirm a qualifying event under IRC §402(e)(4). The events are death, reaching age 59½, separation from service for an employee, or qualifying total and permanent disability for a self-employed participant.
  2. Distribute the entire eligible plan balance within one tax year, normally one calendar year. Include the employer’s other qualified plans of the same type as required. Ask the plan about prior distributions and any required minimum distribution (RMD) before arranging the transfer.
  3. Transfer the eligible employer shares in kind to a taxable brokerage account. Direct-roll eligible cash or mutual fund proceeds to a traditional IRA in the same tax year.
  4. Confirm the stock’s plan cost basis and the reported NUA in box 6 of Form 1099-R. Check which early-distribution exceptions apply and how you’ll pay the upfront tax.

How the comparison works

Both paths use the same annual growth rate for stock and IRA assets. The model assumes you pay the upfront tax from outside cash and values those dollars at the same growth rate through the sale date. The IRA columns value the account after assumed retirement income tax at that date. They represent an after-tax account value, even if you plan to withdraw later.

The estimate applies flat rates to each tax component. It uses the current ordinary rate as a proxy for short-term gains. It excludes state taxes, dividends, fees, capital-loss benefits, progressive brackets, actual IRA withdrawals, IRA early-withdrawal tax, RMD cash flows, and other exceptions to the 10% tax. It assumes all plan assets are pretax and all rolled amounts are eligible.

The basis-to-value guide calls 25% or less low basis and 50% or more high basis. These are screening thresholds. Your rates, timing and projected wealth can change which path comes out ahead.

Frequently asked questions

Can I sell the company stock immediately and still get long-term rates?

Yes. If the distribution qualifies, the gain up to the built-in NUA is long-term capital gain even if you sell immediately. Gains above the distribution value require a holding period of more than one year to receive long-term treatment.

How does the Rule of 55 change the upfront tax?

The Rule of 55 can remove the 10% additional tax on the taxable stock distribution if you separated from the employer sponsoring the plan in or after the year you turned 55. Ordinary income tax on the taxable basis still applies. The Rule of 55 applies to employer plans and does not carry over to traditional IRA withdrawals.

Does the built-in NUA ever owe the 3.8% NIIT on sale?

Realized gain attributable to qualifying built-in NUA is excluded from net investment income even at high income levels. Post-distribution gains may be subject to NIIT. The exemption can still leave the realized NUA in MAGI for purposes of calculating NIIT on other investment income.

What if the stock falls after the distribution?

A sale between your taxable basis and the distribution value still produces long-term NUA gain, limited to the profit you actually realize. A sale below taxable basis produces a capital loss. The calculator assigns no tax benefit to that loss because the benefit depends on your other gains and applicable loss limits.

Can I roll the rest of the plan into an IRA and still use NUA?

Yes, if the distribution otherwise qualifies. Send the employer shares you want to treat as NUA in kind to a taxable account and direct-roll the eligible remaining assets to the IRA. Complete the full distribution in one tax year, including any same-type employer plans that must be counted.
