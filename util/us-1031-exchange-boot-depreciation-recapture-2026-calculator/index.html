How IRC Section 1031 Like-Kind Exchanges Work in 2026

Internal Revenue Code (IRC) Section 1031 remains the cornerstone wealth-compounding engine for US real estate investors. When you sell an investment or business real property—whether an apartment building, commercial warehouse, single-family rental, or bare land—and reinvest the proceeds through a Qualified Intermediary into replacement like-kind real property within strict IRS timelines, you defer four overlapping layers of taxation simultaneously: 25% Unrecaptured Section 1250 Depreciation Recapture, 15% or 20% Federal Long-Term Capital Gains, the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) under IRC §1411, and State Capital Gains Tax (up to 13.3%–14.4% in California or 10.9%+ in New York).

Without a 1031 exchange, selling a $1,200,000 investment property ($72,000 selling costs, $600,000 original cost, $80,000 improvements, $180,000 claimed depreciation) generates a $628,000 realized gain and triggers roughly $189,864 in immediate taxes at the 20% LTCG + 3.8% NIIT + 5% state tier—erasing nearly one-third of your deployable equity. Executing a clean 100% trade-up exchange keeps every dollar of that $189,864 working as down-payment equity inside your next income-producing asset.

Understanding Taxable Boot: Cash Boot vs. Mortgage Boot Netting Rules

To achieve a 100% tax-deferred 1031 exchange, investors must satisfy the classic "Napkin Rule": acquire replacement property whose purchase price is equal to or greater than the net sale price of the relinquished property, AND reinvest 100% of the net equity while replacing equal or greater debt (or adding fresh out-of-pocket cash to cover any debt reduction). Whenever you trade down in price, pull cash out of escrow, or take on a smaller replacement mortgage without injecting fresh cash, you generate taxable "Boot" under Treas. Reg. §1.1031(d)-2:

  • Cash Boot: Any net sales proceeds you pocket at closing—or non-exchangeable closing items paid out of exchange proceeds (such as tenant security deposits, prorated property taxes, or loan origination points)—creates dollar-for-dollar taxable cash boot.
  • Mortgage Boot (Debt Relief): If your relinquished property carried a $450,000 mortgage and your replacement property carries only a $400,000 mortgage, the IRS treats the $50,000 reduction in debt as economic value received ("mortgage boot").
  • Asymmetric Boot Netting Trap: Under Treasury regulations, fresh cash added out of pocket CAN offset mortgage boot (contributing $50,000 of personal cash at closing eliminates $50,000 of mortgage boot cleanly). However, taking on extra replacement debt CANNOT offset cash boot pulled out of escrow! Investors who pull $50,000 of cash out at closing while increasing their mortgage by $100,000 still owe immediate tax on the full $50,000 cash boot.

Why 25% Depreciation Recapture Hits Boot First

A critical ordering rule catches many investors executing a "partial 1031 exchange" by surprise: recognized boot is not taxed proportionally across capital gain and depreciation recapture. Instead, under IRC §1250 and §1(h), Unrecaptured Section 1250 Depreciation Recapture (taxed up to 25% federal, plus 3.8% NIIT and state tax) is recognized FIRST out of every dollar of taxable boot until your entire accumulated depreciation balance has been recaptured. On our default scenario with $180,000 of prior depreciation and $50,000 of mortgage boot, 100% of that $50,000 boot is taxed at the higher 25% recapture tier + 3.8% NIIT + 5% state (33.8% combined = $16,900 tax) rather than the lower 20% LTCG bracket.

Strict 45-Day Identification & 180-Day Closing Deadlines (Zero Extensions)

Both statutory IRS deadlines run concurrently from the exact calendar day your relinquished property closes—weekends and federal holidays included, with virtually zero exceptions outside presidentially declared FEMA disaster extensions:

  1. 45-Day Identification Period: By midnight on Day 45, you must deliver a signed, unambiguous written list of potential replacement properties to your Qualified Intermediary following one of three IRS identification rules: the 3-Property Rule (up to three properties of any value), the 200% Rule (unlimited properties whose combined fair market value does not exceed 200% of the relinquished sale price), or the 95% Rule (acquiring at least 95% of the aggregate value of all identified properties).
  2. 180-Day Exchange Period: You must close on one or more properly identified replacement properties on or before Day 180—or the due date (including extensions) of your federal income tax return for the year of sale, whichever comes earlier! If you close a relinquished sale between October 18 and December 31, you MUST file Form 4868 tax extension on April 15 or your exchange window gets truncated prematurely before Day 180.

Finally, remember the ultimate long-term estate planning synergy known as "Swap 'Til You Drop": although every 1031 exchange reduces your replacement property's carryover tax basis (Replacement Price − Deferred Gain), under IRC Section 1014 your heirs receive a full step-up in basis to fair market value at death, permanently erasing decades of deferred capital gains and 25% depreciation recapture taxes.

Worked 2026 Case Study: Partial Exchange with $50,000 Mortgage Boot

Walk through the exact Form 8824 numbers on our default investment sale: you sell a multifamily duplex for $1,200,000 with $72,000 in brokerage and escrow costs ($1,128,000 net sales price), an original purchase cost of $600,000, $80,000 in capital improvements, and $180,000 in claimed MACRS depreciation. Your adjusted tax basis equals $500,000, creating a $628,000 realized gain. Selling outright without Section 1031 would cost $45,000 in 25% Section 1250 depreciation recapture, $89,600 in 20% federal LTCG, $23,864 in 3.8% NIIT, and $31,400 in 5% state tax—an immediate $189,864 tax hit.

Now suppose you acquire a replacement commercial building for $1,150,000 with a new $400,000 mortgage (down from your old $450,000 loan) without adding personal cash. Even though you bought a higher-priced building than your net sales price, the $50,000 reduction in mortgage debt generates $50,000 of taxable Mortgage Boot. Because depreciation recapture is taxed first, all $50,000 of boot lands in the 25% recapture tier plus 3.8% NIIT and 5% state tax ($16,900 tax due today), while you still successfully defer $578,000 of gain and keep $172,964 in tax dollars compounding inside your replacement property (carryover basis $572,000). Simply bringing $50,000 of fresh personal cash to closing—or increasing the new loan to $450,000—wipes out that $16,900 boot tax completely.

State Clawback Rules & Reverse / Improvement Exchange Options

Investors relocating capital from high-tax coastal states into zero-income-tax states such as Texas, Florida, Tennessee, or Nevada must track state clawback reporting: California (FTB Form 3840), Massachusetts, Montana, and Oregon require annual informational filings tracking deferred in-state gain and collect state tax if the out-of-state replacement property is later sold in a taxable transaction. Additionally, when tight seller timelines prevent closing your relinquished asset first, experienced investors use a Reverse 1031 Exchange (where an Exchange Accommodation Titleholder parks the replacement property for up to 180 days under Rev. Proc. 2000-37) or a Delaware Statutory Trust (DST) fractional interest as a reliable backup identification slot before Day 45 expires.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happens if I take cash out of a 1031 exchange?

Pulling cash out creates taxable 'cash boot' (a partial 1031 exchange). It does not disqualify the entire exchange, but you owe immediate tax on the boot amount—with 25% Unrecaptured Section 1250 Depreciation Recapture taxed first, plus 3.8% NIIT and state capital gains tax.

Can I offset mortgage boot by adding fresh cash at closing?

Yes. Under Treasury Regulation §1.1031(d)-2, contributing fresh out-of-pocket cash toward the replacement property offsets mortgage boot (debt reduction) dollar-for-dollar. However, the reverse does not work: taking on extra new debt cannot offset cash boot pulled out of the sale.

Can I set up a 1031 exchange after my property has already closed?

No. You must engage an independent Qualified Intermediary (QI) and assign the sales contract BEFORE the relinquished property closes. If sales proceeds hit your personal or LLC bank account even for one hour, constructive receipt permanently disqualifies Section 1031 deferral.

Does a primary residence or flip property qualify for a 1031 exchange?

No. IRC Section 1031 applies strictly to real property held for productive use in a trade or business or for investment. Primary residences use IRC Section 121 ($250k/$500k exclusion), and short-term dealer 'flips' held primarily for resale are excluded.
