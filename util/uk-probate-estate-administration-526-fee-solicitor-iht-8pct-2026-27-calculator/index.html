2026/27 UK Probate Fees: The £526 HMCTS Rise and Real Estate Administration Costs

Effective 13 July 2026, HM Courts & Tribunals Service (HMCTS) raised the standard Grant of Probate and Letters of Administration application fee in England and Wales from £300 to £526 for all estates valued above £5,000. Estates valued at £5,000 or less remain exempt (£0 court fee), while additional official sealed copies of the Grant increased to £2 per copy. While the headline £526 court fee grabs media attention, experienced private client solicitors know it is only a tiny fraction of what most bereaved families actually lose during estate administration.

The two genuinely expensive traps in 2026/27 UK probate are (1) signing an open-ended percentage-of-estate solicitor retainer plus 20% VAT on a property-heavy estate, and (2) missing HM Revenue & Customs' strict six-month Inheritance Tax (IHT) payment deadline, after which HMRC charges punitive late payment interest at Bank of England Base Rate + 4% (8.00% per annum as of late 2026).

DIY Probate vs. Fixed-Fee Solicitor vs. Percentage-of-Estate Trap

Executors in England and Wales have three distinct legal routes to obtain a Grant of Probate and administer an estate, and the price difference on a typical £650,000 family home plus savings can easily exceed £8,000 to £15,000:

  • DIY Online Application (HMCTS Portal): Straightforward estates leaving assets to a surviving spouse or children with clear bank accounts and a single UK residential property can often be administered directly by lay executors. Your direct statutory cost is simply £526 + £2 per sealed copy (plus optional Land Registry and Gazette Section 27 notice fees of roughly £250–£300).
  • Fixed-Fee Specialist Probate Solicitor (£1,800–£4,500 + 20% VAT): Many modern SRA-regulated firms offer two transparent fixed-fee tiers: "Grant-Only" service (where the solicitor prepares the IHT400/IHT205 and obtains the Grant while executors close accounts and sell the property) or "Full Administration" for a capped lump sum agreed in writing upfront. On a £650,000 estate, a £2,500 + VAT (£3,000 inc. VAT) fixed quote plus £534 in HMCTS fees totals £3,534.
  • Percentage-of-Estate Billing (1% to 3% + 20% VAT): Traditional high-street banks, trust corporations, and legacy law firms frequently charge an ad valorem percentage of the gross estate—often 1.5% to 2.5% plus 20% VAT, sometimes layered on top of hourly partner rates. On a £650,000 estate, a 1.5% fee equals £9,750 ex VAT, which jumps to £11,700 after 20% VAT—an extra £8,700 over an identical fixed-fee instruction simply because London or South East house prices are high, even though registering one house transfer takes the exact same legal time whether the house is worth £250,000 or £1.2 million.

Executors should also beware of Wills drafted years ago that appoint a bank or law firm as a professional executor. Unless the named firm agrees to renounce probate before intermeddling, it can insist on charging its standard scale tariff. Always request a transparent written fee comparison and confirm whether the named firm will renounce in favour of family beneficiaries when estate complexity is low.

HMRC's 6-Month IHT Deadline and the 8.00% Daily Interest Clock

Under the Inheritance Tax Act 1984, Inheritance Tax is legally due by the end of the sixth month after the month in which the deceased died. For example, if someone passes away on 14 January, the IHT payment deadline is 31 July. Critically, HMCTS will not even issue the Grant of Probate until HMRC issues an IHT421 clearance confirming that the initial IHT payment (or first instalment on qualifying property) has been paid.

Following HMRC's statutory formula uplift to Base Rate + 4 percentage points (replacing the historic Base Rate + 2.5% surcharge), unpaid Inheritance Tax incurs a steep 8.00% annual late payment interest rate—and crucially, HMRC late payment interest is NOT deductible against Inheritance Tax or estate income tax. On £100,000 of unpaid IHT, executors lose £21.92 every single day, compounding to £4,024 in just 180 days of administrative delay. Even if HMRC's probate registry queue takes 12 to 16 weeks, interest continues running relentlessly from day one after the six-month cut-off.

How to Stop the 8.00% Interest Clock: Direct Payment Scheme & 10-Year Instalments

Prudent executors use three legal mechanisms to protect estates from unnecessary HMRC interest while waiting for probate or a slow residential property sale:

  1. HMRC Direct Payment Scheme (DPS via Form IHT423): Most major UK retail banks and building societies participate in the Direct Payment Scheme. Even before probate is granted, executors can submit Form IHT423 instructing the deceased's bank to transfer funds directly from frozen current accounts, savings, or cash ISAs straight to HMRC's IHT account. Making a payment on account before the six-month deadline immediately halts 8.00% interest on the paid balance—even if final property valuations are still pending.
  2. 10-Year Property Instalment Election (IHTA 1984 s.227): For qualifying illiquid assets—primarily residential land, buildings, and certain unquoted business shares—executors can elect on Form IHT400 to pay the attributable IHT in 10 equal annual instalments. The first 10% instalment is due at the six-month deadline with zero interest. However, the remaining nine annual instalments accrue HMRC interest (currently 8.00% p.a.) on the declining unpaid balance. As our calculator shows, keeping £100,000 of property IHT on the full 10-year instalment plan generates roughly £36,000 in non-deductible interest. Consequently, executors normally use the instalment election solely to unlock the Grant of Probate quickly, then immediately clear the remaining balance in full the day the house sale completes.
  3. Executor Loans vs. HMRC 8.00% Rate: If an estate is "asset-rich, cash-poor" (for instance, a £900,000 unencumbered home with only £5,000 in cash), beneficiaries can sometimes lend cash to the estate temporarily, or compare specialist probate bridging loans against HMRC's 8.00% statutory charge. Always check whether the estate qualifies for the full £325,000 Nil-Rate Band (NRB), the £175,000 Residence Nil-Rate Band (RNRB) (up to £1,000,000 combined for married couples/civil partners), or unused transferable allowances from a pre-deceased spouse before making a payment on account.

Essential Disbursements and Compliance Steps Executors Forget

Beyond the £526 HMCTS court fee and legal fees, budget for four routine estate disbursements: (1) RICS Red Book property valuation (£300–£750) to defend probate value against HMRC's Valuation Office Agency; (2) Section 27 Trustee Act statutory notices in The London Gazette and a local newspaper (~£250–£350), which shield executors from personal liability for unknown creditors after two months; (3) bankruptcy searches against beneficiaries (£2–£6 per beneficiary) before distributing funds; and (4) specialist vacant-property probate insurance, as standard home policies usually lapse 30 to 60 days after death. Keeping clean estate accounts from day one prevents beneficiary disputes, avoids personal surcharge risk, and ensures every pound goes to the family rather than avoidable fees or HMRC late interest.

Worked 2026/27 Case Study: £650,000 Estate with £100,000 IHT Liability

Consider an unmarried homeowner in England who dies leaving a £580,000 residential property and £70,000 in cash and investments (£650,000 gross estate). After applying the standard £325,000 Nil-Rate Band (and assuming no Residence Nil-Rate Band applies because the estate passes to nieces and nephews), the taxable estate is £325,000, creating a £130,000 IHT bill (40%). If the executors need four extra official sealed copies of the Grant, the statutory HMCTS court outlay post-13 July 2026 is £526 + £8 = £534.

Now compare the three legal representation paths on that exact estate. A capable family executor filing via the HMCTS online portal pays just £534 in court costs. Instructing a transparent fixed-fee specialist solicitor at £2,500 + 20% VAT (£3,000 inc. VAT) brings total administration cost to £3,534. By contrast, signing a legacy bank or percentage-based firm charging 1.5% of gross estate value plus 20% VAT (£9,750 + £1,950 VAT = £11,700) pushes the bill to £12,234—a completely avoidable £8,700 wealth drain before a single penny of tax is paid.

Worse still, suppose the executors wait until the house sells 12 months after death (180 days past the six-month HMRC deadline) without making a Direct Payment Scheme transfer from the £70,000 cash balance. On £100,000 of unpaid IHT, HMRC's 8.00% daily compounded interest drains £21.92 per day—adding £4,024 in non-deductible penalty interest in six months alone. And if the executors instead leave £100,000 of property IHT on the 10-year instalment option for the entire decade, cumulative non-deductible interest reaches roughly £36,000. Combining a fixed-fee solicitor quote, early Form IHT423 bank transfers, and prompt settlement of property instalments upon completion can routinely preserve £12,000 to £40,000 for beneficiaries.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much is the UK probate application fee in 2026/27?

From 13 July 2026, the HMCTS probate application fee in England and Wales is £526 for estates valued above £5,000 (up from £300 previously). Estates worth £5,000 or less pay £0. Additional official sealed copies of the Grant cost £2 each.

Why is a percentage-based probate solicitor fee usually more expensive than a fixed fee?

Percentage probate quotes (typically 1% to 3% of gross estate value plus 20% VAT) scale automatically with house prices rather than legal workload. On a £650,000 home, a 1.5% + VAT quote costs £11,700, whereas a fixed-fee SRA solicitor handling the exact same paperwork often charges £2,500 to £4,000 + VAT.

When does HMRC start charging 8.00% late payment interest on Inheritance Tax?

HMRC charges late payment interest starting on the first day after the end of the sixth month following the month of death. Because the statutory rate is linked to Bank of England Base Rate plus 4%, unpaid IHT accrues heavy daily interest that is not tax-deductible.

Can I pay Inheritance Tax before probate is granted if the deceased's bank accounts are frozen?

Yes. Executors can use HMRC's Direct Payment Scheme (Form IHT423) to instruct most major UK banks, building societies, and NS&I to release frozen estate cash directly to HMRC to cover the IHT liability before the Grant of Probate is issued.
