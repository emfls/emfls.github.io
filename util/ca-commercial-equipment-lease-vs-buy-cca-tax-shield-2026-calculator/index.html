Commercial Equipment Financing in Canada: Lease vs Buy Decision Matrix (2026 Guide)

Canadian business owners and Chief Financial Officers (CFOs) face a recurrent financial dilemma when expanding operations: should they buy commercial equipment utilizing cash or bank debt, or enter into an operating or capital lease agreement? A correct evaluation requires modeling both nominal cash flows and the Net Present Value (NPV) of after-tax corporate outlays under current Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) rules.

1. Capital Cost Allowance (CCA) and the Accelerated Investment Incentive

When purchasing commercial machinery or technology in Canada, the cost cannot be expensed in full against revenues in Year 1. Instead, the asset is pooled under its designated Capital Cost Allowance (CCA) class:

  • Class 8 (20% declining balance): Miscellaneous office furniture, photocopiers, communication equipment, and general manufacturing tools costing $500 or more.
  • Class 10 (30% declining balance): Motor vehicles, vans, trailers, and general contractor mobile machinery.
  • Class 50 (55% declining balance): General-purpose computer hardware and systems software.

Under the CRA's Accelerated Investment Incentive (AII), the traditional half-year rule is replaced with an enhanced first-year allowance equal to 1.5 times the regular rate. For example, a Class 8 asset receives a 30% deduction in Year 1 rather than the standard 10% under the traditional half-year restriction, front-loading significant tax deductions to maximize early liquidity.

2. Why Net Present Value (NPV) Governs the Decision

Simple nominal comparisons can be deeply misleading. An equipment purchase requires substantial upfront equity capital (or down payment) and debt servicing over time, but leaves the enterprise with salvageable asset value at the end of its useful life. Conversely, leasing spreads disbursements evenly across monthly operational expenditures.

By discounting each year's after-tax net cash outflow by the firm's cost of capital (WACC), NPV quantifies the true economic cost in today's dollars. The alternative yielding the lowest NPV of cost represents the economically superior choice that creates shareholder value.
